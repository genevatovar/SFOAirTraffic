SFO Through Crisis:
A Passenger Story

Geneva Tovar

25 Years of Disruption

SFO is one of the most recognized airports in the United States, known for its museum exhibits, dining, and sensory rooms. But behind that reputation is a passenger traffic story shaped by forces far outside the airport's control. This project traces over 25 years of monthly enplaned passenger data, from the aftermath of 9/11 and the 2008 recession to the near-total collapse of air travel during COVID-19 and the slow climb back. Through four visualizations, it explores how deep each disruption cut, whether domestic and international routes recovered at the same pace, which airlines grew or pulled back, and what seasonal patterns look like when you zoom out across two and a half decades.

01 — Overview

Seeing the Full Picture

Each cell is one month at SFO — darker means more passengers. Summer peaks repeat like clockwork every year, with July and August consistently the busiest months across all 25 years. When disruptions hit, the pattern breaks. 9/11 caused a sharp but brief dip in 2001, with traffic recovering within two years. The 2008 recession shows a slower fade — cells gradually lighten across 2008 to 2010 before rebuilding. COVID-19 is in a category of its own: nearly the entire 2020 row goes white, representing a near-total collapse of air travel that no prior event came close to matching. Recovery is visible through 2022 to 2024, but the rows haven't yet reached the deep blues of 2018 and 2019.

02 — Global

Where SFO Connects

Asia and Europe have anchored SFO's international reach for over two decades, together accounting for the majority of outbound international passengers in every era. The pre-9/11 snapshot from 2000 to 2001 shows what a stable baseline looked like, with Asia already the dominant share reflecting the Bay Area's Pacific Rim ties. The post-9/11 years from 2002 to 2007 show a modest but visible contraction, particularly on long-haul international routes, before traffic rebuilt heading into the late 2000s. The recession years show another contraction across all regions, though carriers pulled back capacity rather than abandoning routes entirely. The pre-COVID peak from 2011 to 2019 saw Asia's share grow even further as routes to China, Japan, and South Korea expanded. COVID tells a different story. International travel collapsed across every region, with Middle East and Central America routes nearly disappearing entirely. Recovery has been uneven. Asia has rebuilt but has not returned to 2019 levels. Some smaller regional routes that existed before 2020 have not come back at all.

03 — Recovery

Domestic vs. International

The jagged shape of both lines reflects real seasonality: summer months peak every year as vacation travel surges, then dip in winter. That rhythm held consistently from 2000 through 2019 across both domestic and international routes. When disruptions hit, the two segments responded very differently. After 9/11, domestic traffic recovered within about a year. International routes took longer, weighed down by ongoing security concerns and reduced international demand. The 2008 recession shows a gentler but sustained dip in both panels, with international traffic slightly more affected as discretionary long-haul travel dropped first. COVID-19 is the exception that makes every other disruption look minor. Both lines collapse in early 2020, but the recovery tells the real story. Domestic travel began climbing again by mid-2021, driven by pent-up demand and the reopening of domestic routes. International traffic stayed flat well into 2022, held back by travel bans, closed borders, and reduced airline capacity on long-haul routes. By 2023 and 2024 both lines are trending upward, but international passenger counts have still not returned to the record highs seen in 2018 and 2019, making clear that the two markets are recovering at fundamentally different rates.

04 — Airlines

Who Grew, Who Pulled Back

United Airlines has dominated SFO for the entire 25-year period, consistently accounting for the largest share of enplaned passengers. Its position reflects SFO's role as a United hub, which gives it structural advantages that other carriers cannot easily replicate. Below United, the landscape has shifted considerably. After 9/11, several smaller carriers pulled back capacity at SFO and never fully returned, leaving more room for the airlines that stayed. The 2008 recession caused a broad contraction across all carriers, but the relative order held. COVID-19 was a different kind of disruption entirely. The total volume collapsed in 2020, and when recovery began, not all airlines came back at the same rate. Alaska Airlines maintained a relatively strong presence throughout recovery, while some international carriers that had operated niche routes out of SFO reduced or eliminated their footprint. The "Other" category, which captures all airlines outside the top six, shrank noticeably after COVID, suggesting that smaller carriers exited or reduced SFO operations more than the major carriers did. By 2024 the total volume is approaching pre-COVID levels, but the composition of airlines flying out of SFO looks meaningfully different than it did in 2019.
